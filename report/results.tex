\section{Results}

The benchmark instances used in the experiments were the provided LOP instances of sizes 150 and 250. Each algorithm was applied once to each instance, and a random seed was set for reproducibility and fair comparaison.
For each run, two values were recorded:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item the final solution cost
    \item the computation time
\end{itemize}

Below is a summary table, where solution quality is measured using the relative percentage deviation from the best known solution, defined as
\[
\Delta_{ki} = 100 \cdot \frac{\text{best-known}_i - \text{cost}_{ki}}{\text{best-known}_i}.
\]

With this convention, smaller values correspond to better solutions.

Table~\ref{tab:ex1} highlights several trends. First, the insert neighborhood gives the best overall solution quality, since the average deviations are the lowest among the three neighborhoods. Second, the effect of initialization method depends strongly on the neighborhood. So this suggests that when the neighborhood is weak, the choice of initialization method has a strong impact on the solution quality. In contrast the deviation values for the exchange and insert neighborhoods are much closer, which suggests that the neighborhood is strong enough to compensate the poor initial solution. 

Third, the comparaison of pivoting rules shows that best-improvement does not always give better results than first-improvement. For the insert neighborhood, the first-improvement achieve slightly better results than best-improvement, both for random and CW initialization. Same thing happens for the exchange neighborhood, with first-improvement again producing smaller deviations than best-improvement.

Finally, in terms of computation time, the transpose neighborhood is by far the fastest, since it only involves swapping adjacent elements and has a small neighborhood size. The exchange and insert neighborhoods are more computationally expensive, but the speed comes with poor solution quality. Exchange and insert have higher computation times, especially for the first-improvement variants, but do yield better results.

Between all the neighborhoods, insert offers the best compromise between solution quality and computation time, which makes it the most effective choice in these experiments.

\subsection{Exercise 1.1: Statistical Comparisons}

A series of tests were performed to compare the algorithms, to answer the questions of : is there a statistically significant difference between the solution quality generated by the different algorithms? The t-test and the wilcoxon test are used to compare the results of the two algorithms on the same instances, it works as follows: The null hypothesis \(H_0\) states that there is no significant difference in solution quality between the two algorithms, while the alternative hypothesis \(H_1\) states that there is a significant difference. The test calculates a p-value, which represents the probability of observing the data if the null hypothesis were true. If the p-value is less than a chosen significance level (here we chose 0.05 like in the lecture example), we reject the null hypothesis in favor of the alternative. We use both tests because they rely on different assumptions. The paired t-test is a parametric test and focuses on differences in the mean, whereas the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is non-parametric and focuses on the median of the paired differences. The lecture example showed that the two tests may lead to different conclusions on the same data, which is why reporting both provides a more complete statistical analysis.

Table~\ref{tab:stats_ex1} highlights several important results. First, the comparisons between best-exchange and best-insert are highly significant both with random initialization and with CW initialization. Since the insert variants achieved smaller average deviations, these results confirm that the insert neighborhood clearly outperforms the exchange neighborhood in terms of solution quality.

Second, the comparison between first-insert random and best-insert random is also statistically significant. In this case, the first-improvement version achieved a smaller average deviation than the best-improvement version, which shows that best-improvement is not necessarily better in practice on these benchmark instances.

Third, the comparison between first-insert random and first-insert CW is not significant. This suggests that when the insert neighborhood is used, the initialization method has little influence on the final solution quality.

Finally, the comparison between best-transpose random and best-transpose CW is highly significant. Since the CW version is much closer to the best known solutions, this indicates that initialization is particularly important when the neighborhood is weak, as is the case for transpose.

\subsection{Exercise 1.2: Statistical Comparison}

The two VND variants were also compared using paired Student t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Average deviation vnd-tei: \(-2.0633\)
    \item Average deviation vnd-tie: \(-1.9959\)
    \item Std deviation vnd-tei: \(0.4191\)
    \item Std deviation vnd-tie: \(0.4220\)
    \item Paired t-test p-value: \(0.2136\)
    \item Wilcoxon p-value: \(0.4169\)
\end{itemize}

The transpose--insert--exchange variant obtained a slightly better average deviation than the transpose--exchange--insert variant, which means that it was slightly better on average. However, both statistical tests returned p-values greater than \(0.05\). Therefore, no statistically significant difference in solution quality was observed between the two VND algorithms. In other words, although \texttt{vnd-tie} appears slightly better empirically, this difference is not strong enough to conclude that it systematically outperforms \texttt{vnd-tei}.
